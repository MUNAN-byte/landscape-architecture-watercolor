# Scene Routing

Classify before composing. Camera distance controls how much identity detail survives; lighting controls where paper white and concentrated pigment belong.

## 1. Camera distance

### Near view: facade, storefront, window display, doorway, architectural detail

Preserve two to four identity cues: dominant frame, opening shape, sign or writing trajectory, display mass, repeated lamps, or signature color block. Replace literal detail with strong geometry and a few calligraphic lines.

For a storefront or illuminated window, use the proven structure-led approach:

- Translate frames and mullions into restrained vertical dark washes.
- Translate lamps and reflections into reserved paper-white circles or pale halos.
- Translate merchandise or display blocks into overlapping watercolor rectangles using the source's dominant color.
- Translate visible writing into loose, non-legible gestural trajectories. Never reproduce branded or incidental text unless explicitly required.
- Use one or two small splatter clusters only if the source contains reflections, dense writing, or visual sparkle.

This mode should feel like a visual memory of geometry, light, handwriting, and color—not a painted shop illustration.

### Mid view: building plus surroundings, street, courtyard, interior volume

Preserve the silhouette, one perspective system, the main opening or roof rhythm, and the relationship between structure and surroundings. Use one dominant architectural mass, one structural dark family, and one atmospheric or vegetation wash. Keep secondary objects subordinate.

### Far view: skyline, mountain-and-building panorama, coast, broad landscape

Prioritize horizon, depth layers, scale, atmosphere, and light. Reduce buildings to grouped masses or sparse vertical rhythms. Use broad wet-in-wet washes and one dark anchor. Avoid evenly describing every mountain, tree, cloud, or building.

## 2. Dominant light

### Soft frontal or overcast light

Use low-contrast transparent layers, fewer hard edges, and temperature shifts. Create hierarchy through overlap and density rather than outlines.

### Side light

Keep the principal light-shadow division and one concentrated dark anchor. Reserve lit planes as paper or very pale wash; let shadow masses merge.

### Backlight, mist, or glare

Reserve paper white for the light source and rim light. Simplify subjects toward silhouettes, soften distant edges, and do not invent shadow detail.

### Artificial light, storefronts, and interiors

Extract luminous points, repeated fixtures, reflections, frames, and saturated display colors. Combine geometric blocks with calligraphic lines. This is the preferred branch for the approved storefront direction.

### Golden hour or colored ambient light

Use the dominant light temperature as the pale unifying wash. Place the complementary or neutral dark only where the source supports structural contrast.

## 3. Subject-specific watercolor languages

- **Glass and reflections:** broken overlapping washes, interrupted axes, paper-white light points; avoid painting every reflected object.
- **Masonry and facades:** simplified masses with dry-brush interruptions only at focal edges; avoid brick-by-brick texture.
- **Water and coast:** horizontal transparent bands, reserved highlights, a few broken strokes; avoid repeated decorative wave symbols.
- **Mountains and mist:** layered value and temperature washes with edge loss; include one decisive dark or distinctive contour so the result is not generic.
- **Trees and foliage:** grouped organic masses with a few directional dry-brush accents; avoid leaf-by-leaf rendering.
- **Night city:** deep connected shadow masses, sparse luminous paper reservations, and limited saturated accents; avoid neon colors not present in the source.

## 4. Mark budget

Choose one primary family:

- overlapping geometric washes;
- broad atmospheric bands;
- grouped organic masses;
- simplified architectural silhouette.

Choose no more than two supporting families:

- structural vertical/horizontal strokes;
- calligraphic trajectories;
- reserved light points;
- sparse blooms, drips, or splashes;
- tiny grouped human marks when scale requires them.

If a supporting family cannot be tied to a source fact, remove it.
